01 · Executive summaryLawrence Bank
In brief

Three things your fee schedule says about you

$50 vs $35international wire vs peer median

Your international wire fee sits near the top of the cohort.At $50.00, your outgoing international wire is at the 90th percentile of 10 peer banks — $15 over the $35 median and above the cohort's 75th percentile of $47.50. International wire customers are disproportionately businesses and internationally connected households, a segment with growing low-cost alternatives that publish their pricing aggressively.

P96deposited item return percentile

Returned deposited items are priced near the top of the market.Your $25.00 deposited-item return fee sits at the 96th percentile of 27 peers — $10 over the $15 median and above the 75th percentile of $20. This fee lands on customers whose deposited check bounced through no fault of their own, which makes a near-top-of-cohort price a reputational bet as much as a revenue line.

-25%outgoing domestic wire vs peer median

You are below market on everyday money movement — quietly.Outgoing domestic wires ($15 vs a $20 median, 47th percentile), incoming wires ($12 vs $15, 41st percentile), and cashier's checks ($5 vs an $8 median, 33rd percentile) all run below the cohort. That is either unclaimed margin or an untold customer-friendly story; today it is neither, because these prices are invisible next to the two lines that stand out for the wrong reason.

Net position: Lawrence Bank prices core penalty fees close to market (NSF at $15 vs a $12 median, 60th percentile) but carries two conspicuous premiums — an international wire at the 90th percentile and a deposited-item return fee at the 96th — alongside quieter premiums on paper statements (88th percentile) and stop payments (80th). At the same time, its everyday domestic money-movement fees run below the median and earn no credit for it. The pattern is a schedule priced line by line rather than as a posture; the three findings above are the agenda for making it deliberate.

03 · What stands outLawrence Bank
Divergences

Where you diverge from your market

International wire (outgoing) — $50.00

Peer median $35.00 · P75 $47.50 · 90th percentile · n=10

The largest dollar gap on your schedule: $15 over the median, near the top of the cohort. This fee falls on business and internationally connected customers who have the most alternatives and the most reason to compare. It deserves either a competitive rationale or a move toward the median.

Deposited item return — $25.00

Peer median $15.00 · P75 $20.00 · 96th percentile · n=27

This fee charges your customer for someone else's bad check. At $10 over the median and above the 75th percentile of a 27-bank cohort, it is the kind of line that attracts junk-fee framing quickly — the triggering event is outside the customer's control. It warrants a decision rather than a default.

Small recurring fees — a quiet premium stack

Paper statement $5.00 vs $3.00 median (P88, n=16) · Stop payment $34.00 vs $30.00 median (P80, n=15)

Individually modest, together these place two routine consumer touchpoints in the top quintile of the cohort. Paper statements in particular hit older and lower-digital-engagement customers monthly — a $2-over-median fee that compounds into a visible pattern on the statement itself.

Domestic wires and cashier's checks — below market, marketed nowhere

Outgoing wire $15 vs $20 median (P47) · Incoming wire $12 vs $15 median (P41) · Cashier's check $5 vs $8 median (P33)

Three everyday service fees priced below most peers. If deliberate, they belong in your messaging — 'cheaper to move money here than at most banks in your market' is a claim your competitors' own published schedules substantiate. If not deliberate, they are margin available without touching a single headline fee.

06 · Market contextLawrence Bank
The bigger picture

What is moving in your market

Two currents define the fee landscape your schedule competes in. First, the retreat from NSF: large banks have largely eliminated returned-item fees, and the pressure has migrated down-market — visible in your own cohort, where the NSF median has fallen to $12 and your own $15 fee sits only modestly above it. Second, deposit competition has turned fee schedules into marketing documents: published fees are public, scraped, and compared, which converts every outlier line into a small reputational bet and every below-market line into an unclaimed advantage.

Community-scale institutions ($1B–$10B) occupy the exposed middle: large enough to be included in comparisons, without the brand gravity that lets money-center banks charge premiums quietly. In that position, the highest-leverage moves are usually not across-the-board cuts but surgical ones — trimming the two near-top-of-cohort fees on international wires and returned deposited items, deciding whether the quiet premiums on paper statements and stop payments are worth their visibility, and actively advertising the money-movement fees already priced below market.

Methodology & data provenance

Fee data is drawn exclusively from institutions' published fee schedules — public documents — collected and verified through the Bank Fee Index agentic pipeline (discovery, extraction, independent verification, publication). Peer cohort: Community banks, $1B–$10B — 86 institutions with published data. Percentiles are computed within-cohort per fee category; a category is flagged an outlier when it exceeds 2× the peer median (extreme) or P75 + 1.5×IQR (statistical). Data pulled Aug 16, 2026. Figures reflect the lowest published consumer-facing amount per category and may not capture account-specific waivers.
