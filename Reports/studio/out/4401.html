01 · Executive summaryTampa Bay Federal Credit Union
In brief

Three things your fee schedule says about you

$5 vs $24.50overdraft fee vs peer median

Your overdraft fee is one-fifth the peer median.At $5.00, your overdraft fee sits at the 17th percentile of 6 comparable credit unions, against a $24.50 median and a $30 maximum. That is a genuinely member-friendly price on the industry's most scrutinized fee — and one of the strongest competitive claims a credit union can make right now, if members actually hear about it.

$10 vs $35card replacement vs peer median

Your service fees run far below comparable credit unions.Card replacement ($10 vs a $35 median, 26th percentile of 23 peers) and stop payments ($5 vs a $25 median, 17th percentile) are priced at a fraction of what comparable credit unions charge. Together with the $5 overdraft, this is a schedule built around the member — either by design or by drift. If by design, it is an untold story; if by drift, it is margin left unexamined.

$35 vs $32deposited item return vs peer median

You are priced at or below market almost everywhere — with one exception.Across the schedule, your fees sit at or under the peer median. The exception is the deposited-item return fee: $35 against a $32 median, tied for the highest in a small 5-peer sample. It is a modest $3 gap, but it is the only line where your schedule tops the cohort — worth a deliberate look precisely because it breaks an otherwise member-friendly pattern.

Net position: Tampa Bay Federal Credit Union prices at or below market on nearly every published fee — most dramatically on overdraft ($5 vs a $24.50 peer median), stop payments ($5 vs $25), and card replacement ($10 vs $35). NSF sits at the cohort midpoint ($15 vs a $17.50 median). The one line above market is the $35 deposited-item return fee, a $3 gap at the top of a small peer sample. The strategic question here is not what to cut; it is whether a schedule this member-friendly should remain a secret, and whether the below-market prices are intentional positioning or unpriced drift.

03 · What stands outTampa Bay Federal Credit Union
Divergences

Where you diverge from your market

Overdraft — $5.00, one-fifth the peer median

Peer median $24.50 · P75 $29.00 · 17th percentile · n=6

Overdraft pricing is the most publicly scrutinized line on any fee schedule, and yours is among the lowest in your cohort. Comparable credit unions charge up to $30. This belongs in member communications and new-member marketing: it is a concrete, verifiable proof point of the credit-union difference, substantiated by your peers' own published schedules.

Service fees — stop payment $5, card replacement $10

Stop payment $5 vs $25 median (P17, n=6) · Card replacement $10 vs $35 median (P26, n=23)

Both fees run at a fraction of the cohort. If these prices are deliberate, they are marketing assets going unused. If they have simply not been revisited, note that even a move to $10 and $20 respectively would still leave you below the peer median — the rare case where a price review can raise revenue without surrendering the member-friendly position.

Deposited item return — $35.00

Peer median $32.00 · peer max $35.00 · n=5

This is the one fee where you sit at the top of the cohort, tied with the highest peer in a 5-institution sample. The dollar gap is small, but the fee lands on members whose deposited check bounced through no fault of their own — a category that draws junk-fee framing quickly. Given that everything else on your schedule tells a member-first story, this line is worth aligning with the rest of it.

06 · Market contextTampa Bay Federal Credit Union
The bigger picture

What is moving in your market

Two currents define the fee landscape your schedule competes in. First, the industry-wide retreat from NSF and overdraft fees: regulatory and reputational pressure that began with the largest banks has migrated to community institutions, compressing penalty-fee medians across the market — a trend your $5 overdraft and $15 NSF already sit comfortably inside. Second, published fee schedules have become marketing documents: fees are public, scraped, and compared, which means every below-market line on your schedule is a competitive claim you can prove — and every above-market line, however small, is visible to anyone who looks.

Mid-sized credit unions occupy an exposed middle: large enough to appear in fee comparisons alongside banks and larger credit unions, without the marketing budgets that let bigger institutions shape their own narrative. For an institution priced the way you are, the highest-leverage move is not a pricing change at all — it is telling the story. A member-first fee schedule that members have never seen compared to the alternatives is doing only half its job; the single above-market exception is a quick fix that makes the story airtight.
