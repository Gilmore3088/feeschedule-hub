01 · Executive summaryHomeTrust Bank
In brief

Three things your fee schedule says about you

$25 vs $15incoming domestic wire vs peer median

You charge the most in your cohort to receive money.At $25.00, your incoming domestic wire fee is a statistical outlier — the 100th percentile of 17 peer banks, $10 over the $15 median and above the entire cohort's 75th percentile of $15. Wires arrive from your most attractive customers: businesses and high-balance households. This is a friction fee on exactly the relationships you want more of.

P96deposited item return percentile

Returned deposited items are priced near the top of the market.Your $25.00 deposited-item return fee sits at the 96th percentile of 27 peers — $10 over the $15 median. This fee lands on customers whose deposited check bounced through no fault of their own, which makes a top-of-cohort price a reputational bet, not just a revenue line.

$5 vs $12your NSF fee vs peer median

Your customer-friendly fees are a story nobody is telling.Your NSF fee ($5 vs a $12 median, 31st percentile), free card replacement ($0 vs a $10 median), and $1.50 paper statement — the lowest of 16 peers — add up to a genuinely below-market consumer fee posture. That is marketing material your competitors' own published schedules substantiate, and today it earns you nothing.

Net position: HomeTrust Bank runs a split fee schedule. On consumer-visible penalty and service fees it is at the friendly end of the market — a $5 NSF fee, free card replacement, and the cheapest paper statement in its cohort. On money-movement and exception fees it sits at or near the top: the highest incoming domestic wire fee of 17 peers (a flagged statistical outlier) and a 96th-percentile deposited-item return fee. The exposure is asymmetrical: the two outliers draw the scrutiny while the below-market fees go unadvertised. Both halves of that split are decisions worth making deliberately.

03 · What stands outHomeTrust Bank
Divergences

Where you diverge from your market

Incoming domestic wire — $25.00

Peer median $15.00 · P75 $15.00 · 100th percentile · n=17 · flagged: statistical outlier

No peer in the cohort charges more to receive a domestic wire. At $10 over the median it earns little revenue but sits alone at the top of the distribution — the kind of line item that surfaces in commercial-relationship negotiations and side-by-side fee comparisons. A cut to the $15 median would be nearly invisible in revenue and highly visible in positioning.

Deposited item return — $25.00

Peer median $15.00 · P75 $20.00 · 96th percentile · n=27

This fee charges your customer for someone else's bad check. At $25 you are $10 over the median and above the 75th percentile of a 27-bank cohort. Because the triggering event is outside the customer's control, this category attracts junk-fee framing faster than most — it deserves either a reduction or a documented rationale.

NSF, card replacement, paper statements — below market, unmarketed

NSF $5 vs $12 median (P31) · Card replacement $0 vs $10 median (P11, waived) · Paper statement $1.50 vs $3.00 median (P6)

Three consumer-facing fees run meaningfully below your cohort, including a fully waived card-replacement fee that most peers still charge. 'Lower everyday fees than most banks in your market' is a claim your competitors' published schedules prove for you. If these prices are deliberate, put them in front of customers; if not, they represent margin available without touching a headline fee.

Outgoing domestic wire — $25.00

Peer median $20.00 · P75 $25.00 · 80th percentile · n=15

Combined with the incoming-wire outlier, both directions of domestic wire traffic price in the top quintile of your cohort. Individually defensible, together they give wire-active customers — typically businesses — a consistent premium-price experience on money movement.

06 · Market contextHomeTrust Bank
The bigger picture

What is moving in your market

Two currents define the fee landscape your schedule competes in. First, the retreat from NSF: large banks have largely eliminated returned-item fees, and the pressure has migrated down-market — visible in your own cohort, where the NSF median ($12) keeps falling and a meaningful share of peers now charge $5 or less. Your $5 NSF fee already sits on the right side of that trend. Second, deposit competition has turned fee schedules into marketing documents: published fees are public, scraped, and compared, which converts every outlier line into a small reputational bet — and every below-market line into an unclaimed advantage.

Community-scale institutions ($1B–$10B) occupy the exposed middle: large enough to be included in comparisons, without the brand gravity that lets money-center banks charge premiums quietly. In that position, the highest-leverage moves are usually surgical — retiring or trimming the one or two conspicuous outliers, here both concentrated in money-movement fees, while actively advertising the everyday fees already priced below market.

Methodology & data provenance

Fee data is drawn exclusively from institutions' published fee schedules — public documents — collected and verified through the Bank Fee Index agentic pipeline (discovery, extraction, independent verification, publication). Peer cohort: Community banks, $1B–$10B — 86 institutions with published data. Percentiles are computed within-cohort per fee category; a category is flagged an outlier when it exceeds 2× the peer median (extreme) or P75 + 1.5×IQR (statistical). Data pulled Aug 16, 2026. Figures reflect the lowest published consumer-facing amount per category and may not capture account-specific waivers.
